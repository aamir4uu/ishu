# The Delivery SOP That Lets a 3-Person Agency Run Like a 10-Person One

An agency delivery SOP is a fixed sequence of stages, gates and templates that every client website moves through. It works because it removes repeat decisions, not because it adds process. Three people who never re-argue how a project runs get roughly the throughput of a much larger team that does.

## Key Takeaways

- Small agencies lose capacity to decisions, not to work. Every project that starts from a blank page costs you a planning conversation you have already had.
- A delivery SOP is five stages with a gate between each one. Nothing moves forward until the gate condition is met.
- Two review rounds. Written into the proposal, priced into the quote, enforced in the client portal.
- Three documents carry the whole thing: a scope sheet, a build checklist and a launch checklist. If you need a fourth, your SOP is too complicated to survive a busy month.
- PMI's 2018 Pulse of the Profession found that 52% of projects experienced scope creep, up from 43% five years earlier. Most of that creep enters through the review stage, which is the stage almost nobody writes rules for.

## Why Three People Feel Like One and a Half

You already know the arithmetic doesn't work. Three people, forty hours each, and somehow you're shipping what feels like one and a half people's worth of websites.

Here's where it goes.

A ten-person agency has a project manager who owns the schedule, a designer who owns the visual system, a developer who owns the build, and someone who owns the client relationship. Those roles are boring and specific, and that's the point. Nobody in a ten-person shop wakes up wondering whose job the sitemap is.

In a three-person agency, all four of those roles live inside the same three heads. So every project starts with a negotiation. Who's taking this one? Do we do a wireframe first or go straight to the template? Are we writing the copy or is the client? When does it go live?

None of that is billable. All of it is exhausting. And because nothing you decided last time got written down anywhere the three of you would think to look, you sit down and do the whole negotiation again on the next project.

That's the decision tax. It's the real reason your capacity is lower than your headcount suggests, and it is almost entirely recoverable.

## What an Agency Delivery SOP Actually Is

An agency delivery SOP is not a policy document. It is not a values statement, and it is definitely not the 40-page PDF somebody's ops consultant sold them in 2019 that nobody has opened since.

It's a sequence. Five stages. A gate between each one. A named owner and a named artifact per stage.

That's it.

The gate is the part that does the work. A gate is a condition, not a meeting. Stage two doesn't start because you feel ready for it to start. It starts because the stage one artifact exists and is signed. When the condition isn't met, the project waits, and the waiting is visible to everybody including the client.

Agencies that skip the gates and keep the stages end up with a Gantt chart nobody follows. Agencies that keep the gates get their evenings back.

## The Five Stages of the Delivery SOP

Read these as a template, not scripture. The stage count matters more than the stage names. Five is enough to cover a website project and few enough that you can hold it in your head on a Friday afternoon.

### Stage 1: Scope Lock

Owner: whoever sold the project.

You are not designing anything yet. You are producing one document, the scope sheet, and getting it signed before a single template gets opened.

The scope sheet answers six questions. How many pages, named individually and listed. Who supplies the copy for each one. Who supplies the images. Which integrations are in, which are explicitly out. How many review rounds are included. What the go-live date is, and what happens to it when content arrives late.

That last one is the clause that saves you. Write it plainly: the launch date moves by one working day for every working day that content is late. Clients accept this without argument when it's in the proposal. They fight it bitterly when you raise it in week six.

Gate: signed scope sheet. No signature, no build.

### Stage 2: Build Kickoff

Owner: whoever is building.

Template selected, project created, content dropped into a structured folder or straight into the client portal. Your build should not start with a blank canvas, and if it does, that's your first SOP fix.

Pick four or five templates you know well and standardise on them. Not because variety is bad, but because by the fifth time you build on the same starting point you are moving at roughly double the speed and you have stopped making the two or three mistakes that starting point used to invite. Agencies that let every project start from scratch pay a tax on every project.

Set the build window here and don't move it. Ten working days is a realistic default for a small business site once content is in hand. Publish that number to the client at kickoff.

Gate: all client-supplied content received. Missing content means the clock hasn't started, and the client can see that in their portal without you having to send the email.

### Stage 3: Internal QA Before the Client Sees Anything

Owner: whoever didn't build it.

This is the stage everybody cuts when they're busy, and cutting it is the single most expensive thing a small agency does. A client who finds a broken contact form doesn't simply report a broken contact form and move on with their afternoon. They start looking for other things. They start reviewing your work instead of reviewing their content, and you've lost a round.

So somebody who wasn't in the build opens the site cold and works the checklist. Forms submit and the notification lands. Every internal link resolves. Meta titles and descriptions are written for every page, not auto-generated. Images are compressed and have real alt text. The site loads properly on a phone, an actual phone, not a browser window you dragged narrow. Analytics is firing. SSL is active. The 404 page exists and doesn't look like a crash.

Twenty to thirty minutes. Every project. No exceptions, including the small ones.

Gate: checklist complete and signed off by a second person.

### Stage 4: The Bounded Review Round

Owner: whoever holds the client relationship.

Two rounds. Say it in the proposal, say it at kickoff, say it again in the email that shares the preview link.

Round one is comprehensive. The client sees everything and gives you everything, in one place, within five working days. Round two is corrections only, and it's for fixing what you got wrong in round one, not for introducing what they thought of afterwards.

The mechanism matters more than the rule. If feedback arrives as three emails, two voice notes and a phone call, you will lose track, you'll miss items, and the round will bleed into a third. Feedback needs one channel and it needs to be attached to the thing being discussed. A client marking up the page directly through a feedback tool gives you an unambiguous list. A client describing the header in a WhatsApp message gives you a guessing game.

Anything outside the signed scope gets quoted, not absorbed. This is the discipline that separates agencies with margin from agencies with revenue. Write the quote, send it the same day, and keep it small and specific enough that saying yes is easier than arguing about whether it should have been included. Most clients say yes. The ones who don't at least stop asking.

That PMI figure earlier is worth sitting with: [52% of projects experience scope creep](https://www.pmi.org/-/media/pmi/documents/public/pdf/learning/thought-leadership/pulse/pulse-of-the-profession-2018.pdf), and the number climbed nine points in five years. It doesn't climb because clients got greedier. It climbs because review stages got vaguer.

Gate: written sign-off. An email saying "looks good, go ahead" counts. A verbal yes on a call does not.

### Stage 5: Launch and Handover

Owner: whoever built it.

Domain connected, SSL verified, redirects mapped from the old URLs, sitemap submitted, analytics confirmed receiving data. Backups scheduled. Then the part most agencies forget: hand something over.

A handover pack is one page. Where the site lives, who to contact, what's covered by the maintenance retainer and what isn't, how to request a change. Send it with the launch email.

It takes fifteen minutes and it prevents the slow bleed of tiny unbilled requests that turns a profitable project into a break-even one over the following year. It also gives you a natural place to mention what comes next, whether that's an ongoing SEO retainer or scheduling their social posts through SocialBee.

Gate: handover pack delivered and the project marked closed. Closed means closed. If they come back in March, that's a new job with a new number.

## The Three Documents That Carry the Whole System

Your SOP lives in three files. Not ten. Three.

The scope sheet is one page: the six questions from stage one, plus a signature line. It's the document that makes stage four survivable, because "is this in scope?" stops being an argument and becomes a lookup.

Your build checklist is the stage three QA list rendered as checkboxes, sitting in whatever your team actually opens during a build. Add to it every time something breaks in production. That's the whole maintenance routine, and it's why the checklist stays worth opening. A list that never grows is a list nobody has learned anything from.

Then the launch checklist. Domain, SSL, redirects, sitemap, analytics, backups, handover pack. Ten or twelve items, same every time, and identical whether the client paid two thousand or twenty.

Three is a deliberate ceiling. Each extra template is one more thing to maintain, one more thing that quietly drifts out of date, one more thing somebody skips in a bad week. So when you feel the urge to add a fourth, check whether it belongs inside one of the three you've got.

## How Do You Stop Revision Rounds From Eating Your Margin?

You cap them, you channel them, then you price whatever spills over the cap. Leave any one of those out and the other two stop working.

Capping without channelling fails because feedback scattered across email and phone calls can't be counted, so nobody can tell which round they're in. Channelling without pricing fails because clients keep asking and you keep saying yes. Pricing without capping fails because there's no baseline to price against.

The practical version looks like this. Two rounds in the proposal. One feedback channel, attached to the site itself. Every out-of-scope request quoted within twenty-four hours as a small fixed-price add-on. And a written sign-off at the end of round two that closes the build.

One more thing that sounds petty and isn't: give the client a deadline for their feedback, not just yourself. Five working days. If it hasn't arrived by then, the project pauses and re-enters your queue when it does. Agencies hold themselves to dates and let clients drift, and that asymmetry is where small teams quietly lose weeks.

## How Many Clients Can a Three-Person Agency Handle?

Concurrently, six to eight builds is realistic once the SOP is running, with two or three in active build at any moment and the rest sitting at a gate.

Without an SOP, the same three people manage three or four before quality drops, because every project in flight is consuming attention rather than sitting in a defined state.

The difference isn't effort. It's that a project waiting at a gate costs you nothing. A project in an undefined state costs you a check-in every single day, and check-ins are how small agencies lose their mornings.

## Rolling This Out in 30 Days Without Pausing Client Work

Don't rebuild your agency. Nobody has a spare month.

Week one is the scope sheet. One page, six questions, signature line. Use it on the next project you sell and on nothing else.

In week two, build your QA checklist by walking through your most recent project and writing down everything you checked. Then write down everything you wish you'd checked. That second list is usually longer, and it's the more useful half.

Week three is the one that pays for the other three: get the two-round review clause into your proposal template and your kickoff email, and set up the feedback channel it depends on.

By week four you're writing the launch checklist and the handover template, then running a single project end to end through all five stages while noting every place you had to improvise.

Those improvisations are your real SOP. Fix them, and run the next project. By project three it stops feeling like process and starts feeling like how you work, which is the only version that survives.

Fair warning: month two is where most of this falls over. Somebody skips a gate because the client is nice and the job is small, and once one gate is optional they all are. Small jobs are exactly where the SOP earns its keep, because small jobs are where the unbilled hours hide.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Should Be Included in an Agency Delivery SOP?

Five stages with a gate condition between each, a named owner per stage, and three templates: a scope sheet, a build checklist and a launch checklist. Anything beyond that is documentation you'll stop maintaining by March.

### How Long Does It Take to Document a Delivery SOP?

Four weeks, at roughly two hours a week, provided you write it alongside live projects rather than in a vacuum. Working from a project in front of you beats working from memory, because memory quietly edits out every bit where you improvised.

### Should a Freelancer Use a Delivery SOP or Is It Only for Teams?

Freelancers benefit more, not less. You have no colleague to catch what you missed, so the checklist is your second pair of eyes. The scope sheet also does something a solo operator badly needs: it lets you say no to an out-of-scope request by pointing at a document rather than at yourself.

### How Do I Get Clients to Respect the Review Rounds?

Put the number in the proposal, repeat it at kickoff, and repeat it in the email that shares the preview link. Clients almost never push back on a limit they were told about three times before it mattered. The ones who push back are usually pushing back on being surprised, not on the limit.

### What Happens When a Client Misses Their Content Deadline?

The launch date moves by one working day for every working day the content is late, and this is written into the scope sheet before anyone signs. Then actually enforce it once. After the first time, content tends to arrive.

### Does an SOP Make the Work Feel Generic?

Only if you've standardised the wrong half. Sequence, checks and admin should be identical every time. Design, copy and strategy shouldn't be touched. Removing decisions about process is how you free up attention for decisions about the work.

### How Do I Handle a Client Who Wants to Skip Straight to the Build?

Show them the scope sheet and tell them it takes twenty minutes to complete. Most enthusiasm to skip planning comes from assuming planning means weeks of discovery. When they see it's one page, the objection usually disappears. If it doesn't, that's useful information about how the rest of the project will go.

### Can I Run This SOP Across Multiple Client Sites at Once?

That's the point of it. Six to eight concurrent projects is manageable for three people once each project sits in a defined state you can see at a glance. Managing them from one platform, with shared team roles and a client portal per project, is what keeps the tenth site from costing more attention than the fifth.
